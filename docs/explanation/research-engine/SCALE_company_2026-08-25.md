# Ten hypotheses: a Florida-facing outbound AI-voice vendor at 1M+ calls/month

Ranked by expected value of the test (impact × probability ÷ cost). The obvious answer — "buy a consent-management and do-not-call scrubbing stack, and multiply $500–$1,500 by call volume to size the exposure" — is named here and deliberately excluded from the ten below, because it is the answer everyone in the room already has.

## 1. The retry logic breaks Florida law even when consent is perfect

**Hypothesis.** More than 15% of the vendor's Florida-destined contacts in any given month receive a 4th or later solicitation call on the same subject inside 24 hours, because dialer retry cadence plus voicemail drops plus number rotation are counted together by the statute.

**Why it matters.** If true, the vendor's largest Florida liability is created by an engineering setting nobody in legal has ever seen, and it is fixed by a config change rather than a consent programme — and the deliverability tactic of rotating caller IDs makes it worse, not better, because [§501.059 caps at three calls per 24 hours "regardless of the phone number used to make the call"](https://www.flsenate.gov/laws/statutes/2021/501.059) and [violations carry actual damages or $500, trebled for willful conduct](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/florida-restrictions-telemarketers.html).

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own call detail records, last 30 days, filtered to Florida area codes and Florida-billed numbers. Method: group by destination number and 24-hour rolling window, count attempts irrespective of originating DID, and separately count voicemail drops as attempts. Pass/fail: fails if more than 5% of contacted numbers show 4+ attempts in any 24-hour window. Cost: one analyst, 4–6 hours, roughly £300.

**Prior.** 0.8 — standard dialer defaults are 3–6 attempts per day per lead, and number rotation is near-universal at this volume, so the two practices collide by construction.

**What would change my mind.** A CDR query showing the dedupe key is the destination number rather than the origin-destination pair, and that voicemail drops already decrement the same counter.

## 2. The recording, not the call, is the criminal exposure

**Hypothesis.** More than 50% of the vendor's Florida calls in the last 90 days began capturing audio before any all-party recording disclosure was delivered and acknowledged, which is a separate and larger liability than the calling statute.

**Why it matters.** [Florida is an all-party consent state under §934.03, where interception without every party's prior consent is a third-degree felony](https://www.recordinglaw.com/party-two-party-consent-states/florida-recording-laws/), and [§934.10 adds civil damages, punitive damages and attorney's fees](https://www.justia.com/50-state-surveys/recording-phone-calls-and-conversations/) — so an AI voice agent, which must capture audio to function at all, creates a second per-call claim that consent to be *called* does not cure, and the vendor's own audio archive is the evidence.

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own stored recordings and transcripts. Method: pull 200 random Florida calls from the last 90 days, measure the elapsed milliseconds between start-of-capture and the first utterance of a recording disclosure, and check whether the agent waits for an affirmative response. Pass/fail: fails if the disclosure is absent, or arrives after capture starts, in more than 5% of the sample. Cost: 8 hours plus a deterministic transcript grader, roughly £500.

**Prior.** 0.75 — speech recognition requires audio from the first frame, so "capture then disclose" is the default architecture of every voice agent stack I am aware of.

**What would change my mind.** Evidence that the platform buffers audio locally and discards it unless consent is given, with a retention log proving discards actually happen.

## 3. The vendor cannot produce the consent record when asked

**Hypothesis.** For a random sample of 50 Florida calls placed last month, the vendor cannot produce within 24 hours the timestamped consent record, the source URL or channel, the IP or device identifier, and the exact disclosure wording the consumer saw, for more than 30% of them.

**Why it matters.** If true, the vendor is not selling calls, it is selling undocumented risk transfer to its customers, and the correct move is to make evidence-grade consent custody the product — the thing a buyer, an insurer and a defence lawyer all pay for — rather than a compliance cost centre.

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own systems and its customers' lead sources. Method: pick 50 calls at random, open a ticket for each demanding the full consent packet, stop the clock at 24 hours, count complete packets. Pass/fail: fails if fewer than 35 of 50 come back complete. Cost: £0 in cash, about 1 day of two people's attention.

**Prior.** 0.7 — the consent typically originates at the customer or a lead vendor upstream of the platform, so the platform holds a boolean flag rather than the underlying evidence.

**What would change my mind.** A working retrieval endpoint that returns the full packet, including the rendered disclosure text as displayed, in under a minute for an arbitrary call ID.

## 4. The exposure is uninsurable and nobody has read the policy

**Hypothesis.** The vendor's current general liability and errors-and-omissions policies exclude statutory-damages telemarketing and privacy claims, meaning its insured limit against a certified Florida class is effectively zero as of today.

**Why it matters.** [Nearly all commercial general liability policies now exclude these claims, and insurers have successfully argued that statutory damages are uninsurable penalties](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/tort_trial_insurance_practice/publications/the_brief/2018-19/spring/insurance-and-tcpa-are-claims-against-telemarketers-covered/); [general liability and errors-and-omissions cover typically does not respond](https://www.possiblenow.com/resources/do-not-call-solutions/do-insurance-companies-cover-tcpa-damages/) — so the balance-sheet event is a coverage denial letter arriving weeks after the complaint, and the decision that changes is whether to buy a specific sub-limit, form a captive, or reprice every Florida contract to self-fund the loss.

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own bound policies and endorsements. Method: a coverage lawyer reads the exclusions and writes one page naming the limit that would actually respond to a Florida statutory-damages class claim. Pass/fail: fails if that number is below one year of modelled expected loss. Cost: 2–3 hours of counsel, roughly £600–900.

**Prior.** 0.85 — this exclusion is close to market standard, and a company of this profile rarely buys the bespoke wording that survives it.

**What would change my mind.** A specific affirmative-grant endorsement naming the Telephone Consumer Protection Act or Florida Chapter 501 with a stated limit and a defence-costs-outside-limits provision.

## 5. A few dozen numbers generate most of the claims

**Hypothesis.** Fewer than 50 destination phone numbers and fewer than 15 plaintiff law firms will account for more than 60% of the Florida claims filed against the vendor and its customers over the next 12 months.

**Why it matters.** If the claim distribution is that concentrated — and [Florida's mini-statute has generated litigation in volume since inception](https://natlawreview.com/article/ftsa-claims-are-still-pouring-florida-mini-tcpa-continues-to-generate-huge-volume), against a national backdrop of [1,807 class actions filed in 2025 and 856 in the first four months of 2026 alone, up 23% year on year](https://tcpaworld.com/2026/06/04/april-tcpa-filings-off-the-chart-330-tcpa-cases-255-tcpa-class-actions-filed-in-april-2026-up-40-from-2025/) — then a suppression list of a few hundred numbers is a materially cheaper control than any platform rebuild, and it can ship this week.

**Test.** Data: public Florida state court e-filing dockets and PACER for the Middle and Southern Districts of Florida. Method: extract plaintiff names and counsel from every telephone-solicitation complaint filed in Florida over 24 months, resolve to phone numbers where pleaded, and match against the vendor's CDRs. Pass/fail: fails if the top 50 numbers account for under 30% of matched contacts. Cost: 15 hours plus docket fees, roughly £1,200.

**Prior.** 0.65 — claim-generation in this area behaves like a small professional cohort rather than a random consumer draw, though the concentration ratio itself is my estimate, not a measured figure.

**What would change my mind.** A docket pull showing more than 300 distinct plaintiffs with no repeat counsel, which would mean the risk is genuinely diffuse and only a systemic fix works.

## 6. The agent will not admit it is a machine

**Hypothesis.** In 100 sampled live Florida calls, the agent fails to disclose that it is artificial within the first 10 seconds in more than 90% of cases, and gives a false or evasive answer to a direct "am I talking to a person?" in more than 20%.

**Why it matters.** [The FCC confirmed on 8 February 2024 that artificial-intelligence-generated voices are "artificial" voices under existing federal rules](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal), and [California's AB 2905 has required a disclosure at the start of such calls since 1 January 2025](https://www.getaira.io/resources/california-ai-voice-disclosure) — so a Florida analogue is a live legislative risk, and the honest answer to the bot question is the single cheapest thing to fix before it becomes mandatory. *The per-call penalty figure circulating for AB 2905 rests on one commercial source and is marked unverified here.*

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own transcript corpus. Method: a deterministic grader — substring match on a disclosure phrase list inside the first 10 seconds, plus a regex for "are you a robot / a real person / a human" and a hand-review of only those turns. Pass/fail: fails if either threshold is breached. Cost: 6 hours of engineering, roughly £400.

**Prior.** 0.6 — disclosure suppresses answer rates, so the commercial incentive points hard away from it, but many platforms already ship a default disclosure prompt.

**What would change my mind.** A grader run showing the disclosure present in over 95% of Florida calls and a truthful bot answer in every flagged turn.

## 7. One upstream carrier removal takes the whole Florida business offline

**Hypothesis.** More than 30% of the vendor's Florida-destined traffic originates through three or fewer upstream providers, at least one of which has a deficient or at-risk certification in the FCC's Robocall Mitigation Database today.

**Why it matters.** [On 25 August 2025 the FCC removed more than 1,200 providers from that database, and downstream carriers were required to stop accepting their traffic by 27 August](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-removes-non-compliant-providers-robocall-mitigation-database) — [removal effectively disconnects a provider from the US phone network](https://www.wiley.law/alert-FCC-Enforcement-Action-Removes-Over-1200-Providers-from-Robocall-Mitigation-Database) — so this is a total-outage risk with roughly 48 hours of notice, controlled by a third party, and the fix is multi-homing origination rather than anything legal.

**Test.** Data: the public FCC Robocall Mitigation Database, plus the vendor's own routing tables. Method: list every originating and intermediate provider carrying Florida traffic, look each up in the database, flag any with stale filings, missing complete mitigation plans or prior cure orders. Pass/fail: fails if any provider carrying over 10% of Florida volume is flagged. Cost: 3–4 hours, roughly £250.

**Prior.** 0.55 — concentration on two or three cheap origination partners is normal at this scale; whether one is currently deficient is close to a coin flip given the 2,411 providers ordered to cure in December 2024.

**What would change my mind.** Routing data showing four or more clean-certification providers each carrying under 25% of Florida volume, with automated failover already tested.

## 8. Most of the vendor's Florida-calling customers are not licensed to call Florida

**Hypothesis.** Fewer than 20% of the vendor's customers placing Florida calls hold a current commercial telephone seller licence with the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services.

**Why it matters.** [Non-exempt businesses selling by telephone in Florida must be licensed and must post security of at least $50,000 before soliciting](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-Resources/Consumer-Rights-and-Responsibilities/Telemarketing), [in the form of a bond, certificate of deposit or letter of credit](https://www.bryantsuretybonds.com/surety-bonds-by-state/florida/telemarketing-bond) — so if the customer base is largely unlicensed, the vendor is providing the instrument for an unlawful act, and the licence check becomes both a defensive gate and a genuinely differentiating feature to sell.

**Test.** Data: the public FDACS licensee list. Method: export the vendor's Florida-calling customer names and matched entity names, cross-match against the licensee register, manually resolve the exemption categories for any that claim one. Pass/fail: fails if under 20% are licensed or provably exempt. Cost: 8 hours, roughly £500.

**Prior.** 0.7 — startup and mid-market buyers of voice automation are unlikely to have discovered a state telemarketing licensing regime before switching the dialer on.

**What would change my mind.** Finding that most customers fall cleanly inside a statutory exemption, which would move this from a liability to a non-issue.

## 9. The compliance budget was built for a rule that no longer exists here

**Hypothesis.** More than half of the vendor's 2026 compliance engineering spend is aimed at federal consent mechanics that the Eleventh Circuit vacated on 24 January 2025, while under 20% is aimed at Florida-specific state law.

**Why it matters.** [The Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's one-to-one consent rule as exceeding statutory authority](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca11/24-10277/24-10277-2025-01-24.html), [and the FCC subsequently repealed it](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/us/insights/blogs/fcc-repeals-one-one-consent-rule-following-eleventh-circuit-decision) — Florida sits inside that circuit, so Florida is now the most permissive federal ground on consent form and simultaneously among the harshest on state grounds, which means the money is being spent on the wrong side of the ledger.

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own 2026 engineering roadmap and its inbound legal correspondence. Method: tag every compliance-tagged ticket and every demand letter received since February 2025 as federal-consent, Florida-statute or recording-statute, and compare the two distributions. Pass/fail: fails if the spend mix matches the claim mix within 20 percentage points. Cost: 4 hours, roughly £250.

**Prior.** 0.5 — the rule was widely built for through 2024 before it was struck down, but a competent team may already have redirected.

**What would change my mind.** A demand-letter tally showing federal consent-form theories still dominate the claims actually arriving.

## 10. The customer indemnity that carries the risk is worthless

**Hypothesis.** In more than 60% of the vendor's signed customer agreements, the party contractually indemnifying the vendor for Florida calling claims has less than $5M in annual revenue, so the indemnity is uncollectable against a single certified class.

**Why it matters.** Every voice platform's risk model rests on pushing liability to the customer who supplied the list and the consent, and if the counterparty cannot pay, the vendor is the deep pocket by default — which turns customer onboarding into a credit underwriting problem, not a sales problem, and argues for a minimum-balance-sheet or bonded-customer requirement on Florida traffic.

**Test.** Data: the vendor's own contract repository plus a commercial credit file such as Dun & Bradstreet or Companies House equivalents. Method: sample 25 agreements weighted by Florida call volume, extract the indemnitor entity and any liability cap, pull a credit file on each. Pass/fail: fails if the volume-weighted median indemnitor has under $5M revenue or no rated credit file. Cost: 10 hours plus data fees, roughly £900.

**Prior.** 0.6 — self-serve and mid-market distribution is the growth engine for this segment, and that customer profile is exactly the one that cannot honour an indemnity.

**What would change my mind.** A contract sample showing Florida volume is concentrated in enterprise counterparties with audited financials and their own insurance certificates naming the vendor.
