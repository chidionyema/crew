Trust — the fatal gaps

Nobody is behind this. The footer says “Who makes this,” the page is titled “About,” and it never names a human. For a new brand selling AI-generated research at £29–199, anonymity reads as exactly the thing sceptical buyers fear: a faceless LLM content mill with a Stripe account. Your entire pitch is “we’re the honest ones” — honesty starts with a name, a face, and a one-paragraph “why I built the engine.” This is the single highest-leverage fix on the site.

Your numbers don’t reconcile. “63 packs” in the hero. “Showing 13 of 52” in the catalogue. “1,168 killed · 145 survived” in the filter log. 1,313 researched on /how-it-works. So: 145 survived but 63 are listed — where are the other 82? A buyer you’ve explicitly trained to audit claims will run this arithmetic in ten seconds and conclude either sloppiness or hidden inventory. Reconcile every number or explain the gap (“145 survived; 63 met the bar for packaging”).

The free sample refutes itself. The Solo Builder’s Warranty Audit is your proof-of-rigour artifact, and its own dossier says: commoditised market, accredited incumbents doing the identical service, defensibility 2/5, automatable 1/5, and a “pushed back” verdict on its core claim. Radical honesty is the brand, fine — but leading with a sample whose honest conclusion is “weak idea, crowded market” teaches visitors that surviving your filter doesn’t mean much. Pick a sample that scores well AND shows the adversarial pass drawing blood on something non-fatal.

The evidence pattern is thin and repetitive. Several checks resolve to “a competitor’s website exists” — and the same source (nwsnagsurveys.com) is cited for two different checks. That last one shouldn’t have survived any legality check, and its presence invites someone to test your kill log against your shelf. Audit every pack against your own six gates before launch.

UX and information architecture

The homepage is doing six jobs — hero, full 63-item catalogue, methodology, sample teaser, skills quiz, email capture — in one endless scroll. The quiz (“What skills do you bring?”) is the most interesting interaction on the site and it’s stranded below the whole shelf, after the browsing decision it should have shaped. Move it to the top as the primary filter; “Newest” is how you think about inventory, not how a buyer chooses. Nobody buys a business idea because it’s recent — they buy by skills, capital, and category. Also: a facet showing “Suits an audience — 2” advertises how thin that slice is; hide facets under ~5. The ⌘K hint is dead weight on mobile. Pricing variance (£29–199) is asserted to track “size of the opportunity” but nothing on the card shows that signal — surface the score or opportunity size that justifies each price, or the spread reads arbitrary. And the email capture appears twice with mismatched labels (“Email me if a pack survives” / “Email me” / “Tell me when one survives”) — one form, one label.

The name

Mumchimp is a Mailchimp pun, and Intuit has a documented history of pursuing “-chimp” marks. Separately, “Mum” signals a mumpreneur/parenting niche the catalogue doesn’t serve, so the name misdirects and carries trademark exposure while doing zero semantic work. If you’re pre-launch, this is the cheapest moment you will ever have to change it. At minimum get a trademark opinion before spending on the brand.

2030s standard

The current product is a zip of markdown — 2015 tech with 2026 research. The upgrades that actually move it a decade forward:

Provenance as infrastructure. “Every claim links to its source” dies to link rot within a year. Archive every citation at publish (Wayback/perma.cc), show live + archived, and sign the dossier so claims are machine-verifiable. “Verified last month” should be a process — scheduled re-runs with visible freshness deltas — not a timestamp.
Agent-native packs. Ship a machine-readable manifest (JSON-LD) alongside the markdown. Increasingly your buyer is a person working through their own AI agent; a pack their agent can ingest, query, and re-verify is a different product class from a download.
Living dossiers. “Re-verify now” and “what changed since I bought” buttons. Research decays; a freshness SLA is a moat nobody in this category has.
The kill log is your real asset. 1,168 documented, sourced failures is unique data. Make it queryable, make each kill an indexable page (“is X a viable business” queries), publish the check rubrics’ hashes so “auditable, not a black box” is literally true.
Sell the engine, not just its exhaust. “Run the filter on your idea, £X” is the obvious flagship product — higher willingness-to-pay, recurring, and every run grows the kill log. The packs then become the demo.
Blocking order: name a human → reconcile the numbers → legality audit of the shelf → verify crawlability → swap the sample → editorial sweep → quiz to the top. The concept deserves the polish; right now the gap between the rigour you claim and the rigour the pages exhibit is the thing a sceptical visitor will find first — because you’ve told them to look.
